# M1-L14 – Irrational and real numbers, approximations {#L14}

**Course section:** M1 – Lower secondary education (Sekundarstufe I)

**Level:** Lower secondary education

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Classification:** Lower secondary education, approximately Year 9 of German schooling · Format: quick revision

## What is this about? {#S1}

Not every number can be written as a fraction of two integers. Some lengths and circle measurements have infinitely many decimal places without a repeating pattern. Such numbers are called **irrational**. Together with the rational numbers they form the **real numbers**, which we represent on an idealised number line. In measurements, calculations and computer programs, however, we generally work with approximations – and need to know how reliable they are.

## Basic idea and illustration {#S2}

A **rational** number can be written as the quotient of two integers with a non-zero denominator; its decimal expansion either terminates or repeats from some point onwards. An **irrational** number cannot be represented in this way and has an infinite, non-repeating decimal expansion. Both types correspond to positions on the mathematical number line. A measuring instrument and a computer can display or store only a limited number of digits, however. We must therefore distinguish between the mathematically **exact value** and its **rounded representation**.

## Main idea 1: Rational and irrational numbers {#S4}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac12&=0.5\\
\frac13&=0.3333\ldots\\
\sqrt2&\approx1.41421356\ldots\\
\pi&\approx3.14159265\ldots
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** “One half is exactly zero point five. One third is zero point three, with the three repeating indefinitely. The square root of two is approximately one point four one four two and so on. Pi is approximately three point one four one five nine and so on.”

- $\frac12$ and $\frac13$ are **rational**, even though $\frac13$ has an infinite decimal expansion: the crucial feature is the repeating pattern.
- $\sqrt2$ is **irrational**: there is no fraction of two integers exactly equal to this root.
- $\pi$ is also irrational. The symbol represents the **exact number**, even though a finite decimal written out in full only approximates it.

The three dots `…` are called an **ellipsis**; in decimal numbers, they indicate that the expansion continues. They are not a mathematical operation.

### Mini-example: Diagonal of a square {#abschnitt-5}

A square has a side length of exactly one metre. By Pythagoras' theorem its diagonal has length $\sqrt2$ metres, approximately $1.4142$ metres. The mathematical value is exactly $\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m}$; a measurement or decimal value such as $1.41\,\mathrm{m}$ is only an approximation. Rounding does not make the number itself rational in the mathematical sense – only **its stated decimal approximation** is rational.

## Main idea 2: Rounding and accuracy {#S5}

When approximating a number in decimal form, we choose how many digits to keep. This is useful when displaying a number or reporting a measurement.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{2}&\approx 1.41421356\ldots\\
\text{to two decimal places: }&1.41\\
\text{to three decimal places: }&1.414
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** “The square root of two is approximately one point four one four and so on. Rounded to two decimal places, it is one point four one; rounded to three decimal places, it is one point four one four.” Under the usual school rounding rule (round half up), the first discarded decimal digit determines whether to round up. Other applications may specify a different rounding convention.

It is important to distinguish between **accuracy** and **precision**: stating many digits does not necessarily mean that a measurement is equally reliable. If a length is measured only to the nearest centimetre, giving eight decimal places would be misleading.

## Main idea 3: Absolute error {#S6}

If we know the greatest permitted deviation of an approximation, we can express this formally:

$$
\lvert x-\hat{x}\rvert\leq\varepsilon
$$

**Read aloud:** “The absolute value of the difference between the exact value x and the approximate value x hat is less than or equal to epsilon.” The **absolute value** removes the sign of the difference; it does not matter whether the estimate was too high or too low when judging the size of the error. $\varepsilon$ stands for a positive tolerance specified in advance, in the same unit as x.

### Mini-example: A rounded measurement {#abschnitt-8}

In a **pure rounding example**, we assume a length of exactly $2.346\,\mathrm{m}$; the display shows it to two decimal places as $2.35\,\mathrm{m}$. A real measurement would also have its own measurement uncertainty. The absolute error is $|2.346-2.35|=0.004\,\mathrm{m}$, or four millimetres. The error is therefore within a tolerance of $0.005\,\mathrm{m}$. This statement applies to the **rounding described**; it does not yet account for errors in the measuring instrument.

When rounding to the **nearest hundredth**, the absolute rounding error is at most half a hundredth, or $0.005$ in the unit concerned, regardless of sign. This bound concerns only the rounding of a value assumed to be given. Whether ties are rounded up or down depends on the chosen rounding rule.

## Main idea 4: Why computers do not simply calculate with exact decimal numbers {#S7}

Many programming languages use **binary floating-point numbers** (often formats based on IEEE 754). With a finite number of bits, certain decimal fractions such as $0.1$ or $0.2$ cannot be represented exactly. The stored values are very close to the intended values, but not exactly the same. Consequently, a calculation such as `0.1 + 0.2` may display more digits than expected.

Formula, sketch or calculation scheme · Original notation (German decimal commas in the first calculation)

    # Schematic: mathematical calculation
    0,1 + 0,2 = 0,3

    # Typical binary floating-point output (e.g. JavaScript)
    0.1 + 0.2  →  0.30000000000000004

**Read aloud:** “Mathematically, one tenth plus two tenths is exactly three tenths. Because of its number representation, a computer may display a value very slightly above three tenths.” The **decimal point** rather than the decimal comma is used in the program code; the original German mathematical notation uses a comma, whereas this English text uses a point.

This does not prove that $0.1+0.2$ is mathematically wrong. In programs, the result depends on the number type, rounding method and output behaviour. For financial amounts, for example, integers in cents or suitable decimal types are used, depending on the task. In scientific calculations, we use controlled tolerances instead of unchecked equality comparisons of floating-point results.

## What is it useful for? {#S8}

These distinctions are central to data analysis, numerical algorithms, simulations and AI. An optimisation program needs to know when two results are “close enough”; a measurement application needs sensible tolerances and units. Large vector and matrix calculations also often use limited precision, so rounding errors may grow under unfavourable conditions. In later numerical mathematics, we learn to assess such errors systematically.

## Assumptions and limits {#S9}

An infinite decimal expansion is **not automatically irrational**: $\frac13$ is the counterexample. An approximation does not replace an exact mathematical value. Nor is the number of decimal places displayed proof of measurement accuracy on its own. The upper error bound for rounding describes only the rounding step, not an unknown initial measurement or modelling error. When rounding to a fixed number of places, a rule is also needed for exact halfway cases; the bound $0.005$ assumes rounding to the nearest hundredth. Statements about binary floating-point numbers do not apply unchanged to every programming language and number type.

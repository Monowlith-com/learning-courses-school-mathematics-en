# M2-L17 – Abitur overview: three ways of thinking, one mathematical language {#L17}

**Course area:** M2 – Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Level:** Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Upper secondary education (Gymnasium), including marked advanced-course (Leistungskurs) and technical extensions.

## What is this about? {#S1}

Upper secondary mathematics is not a collection of unrelated calculation recipes. Three major topic areas complement one another: **calculus** studies change, **analytical geometry** describes spatial relationships, and **probability and statistics** model chance and uncertainty. Each area has characteristic questions and its own language of formulae. The aim of this final lesson is to recognise formulae while reading and choose the appropriate mathematical way of thinking, not to recreate an Abitur examination.

## The most important symbols before the summary formulae {#S3}

The symbols are familiar from previous lessons; here they form a reference sheet that can be read directly. The same symbols may be used differently in other subjects.

| Symbol | How to read it | Meaning here | Other possible uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| $f(x)$ | 'f of x' | Function value for input x | Any input-output mapping |
| $\lim$ | 'limit' | Value that expressions approach | Limits of sequences and series too |
| $x\to a$ | 'x tends to a' | The input approaches a | Arrows also occur in function mappings |
| $f'(x)$ | 'f prime of x' | Derivative or local rate of change | A prime may indicate a different variant elsewhere |
| $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$ | 'd y by d x' | Derivative of y with respect to x | Notation also used later in differential equations |
| $\int$ | 'integral' | Continuous accumulation | Indefinite or definite integral |
| $\mathrm{d}x$ | 'd x' | Integration variable or differential notation | Differential in higher calculus |
| $a$, $b$ on the integral | 'lower' and 'upper limit' | Limits of the integration interval | General variables |
| $\vec{v}$ | 'vector v' | Directed quantity | Often a velocity vector |
| $\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$ | 'magnitude/length of vector v' | Euclidean length | Other norms at university level |
| $\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}$ | 'scalar product of u and v' | Calculates a number from two vectors | A dot also denotes ordinary multiplication |
| $P(A)$ | 'probability of A' | Probability of an event | P may denote a point |
| $P(A\mid B)$ | 'probability of A given B' | Conditional probability | A vertical bar can have other meanings |
| $E[X]$ | 'expected value of X' | Mean of a distribution in the model | E may also be another variable |
| $\sum$ | 'capital sigma' | Sum of the specified terms | Lowercase sigma is usually a measure of spread |
| $\sigma$ | 'sigma' | Standard deviation | General model parameter |
| `≈` | 'approximately equal to' | Rounded or approximate value | Measurement and numerical approximation |

A **subscript** is a lowered label, such as the $i$ in $x_i$ ('x with subscript i'). An **exponent** is raised, such as the $2$ in $x^2$ ('x squared'). Both can refer to mathematical objects but perform different tasks.

## Calculus: change and accumulation {#S4}

A function assigns a value to every permitted input. The derivative describes the local change at a point; the definite integral adds up the rate of change over an interval.

Two central formulae to read:

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
$$

**Read aloud in full:** 'f prime of x is the limit of the quotient of f of x plus h minus f of x, divided by h, as h tends to zero.' The $h$ already familiar from [M2-L3](M2-L3.md) is a change in input that tends to zero and is initially non-zero in the division. This is the **definition** of the (two-sided) derivative at an interior point of the domain, provided the finite limit exists; for the values $h\ne 0$ considered, $x+h$ must also lie in the domain. At boundary points, one-sided derivatives must be defined separately.

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=F(b)-F(a)\qquad\text{if }F'(x)=f(x)
$$

**Read aloud in full:** 'The definite integral of f of x with respect to x from a to b gives the antiderivative F at the upper limit b minus the antiderivative F at the lower limit a.' Capital $F$ denotes an antiderivative whose derivative is $f$; for the simple fundamental theorem we need a continuous function on the interval considered here.

**Mini-example:** A vehicle moves for two seconds at a constant **positive** velocity of three metres per second. It travels $3\,\mathrm{m}/\mathrm{s}\cdot 2\,\mathrm{s}=6\,\mathrm{m}$. With $t$ as time in seconds, integrating the constant velocity function gives the same value. If the direction changes, however, the signed integral initially gives the **displacement**; the total distance travelled requires integrating the **absolute value** of the velocity.

**Limit:** Signed integration of velocity initially gives **displacement**; if direction changes, the actual distance travelled is greater than or equal to the absolute value of the displacement.

## Analytical geometry: position, direction, distance {#S5}

A vector can describe a displacement or direction. Lines and planes arise when reference points are combined with direction vectors and parameters. The scalar product compares directions and is also used to calculate angles.

$$
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}=\lVert\vec{u}\rVert\cdot\lVert\vec{v}\rVert\cdot\cos(\varphi)
$$

**Read aloud in full:** 'The scalar product of vectors u and v is equal to the length of u times the length of v times the cosine of the angle phi between them.' $\varphi$ is read as 'phi'; here it denotes the angle. $\cos$ is the cosine. The angle interpretation requires both vectors to be non-zero.

**Mini-example:** The vectors $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are perpendicular to each other in the plane coordinate system. Their scalar product is zero. If both vectors are non-zero, this shows that the angle between them is 90 degrees.

**Limit:** Points and vectors are not the same: a point denotes a location, whereas a direction vector denotes a displacement. The distinctions between an infinite line and a finite line segment are also essential in collision calculations.

## Probability and statistics: probabilities and uncertainty {#S6}

In a probability model an event is assigned a number between zero and one. Conditional probability describes how this assessment changes with additional information.

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\qquad\text{for }P(B)>0
$$

**Read aloud in full:** 'The probability of A given B is the probability that A and B occur together, divided by the probability of B.' $\cap$ means 'intersection', or for events 'A and B together'. $\mid$ means 'given' here; B must have positive probability.

**Mini-example:** Of 100 equally weighted test files, 10 are faulty. A checking program raises an alarm for 8 faulty and 9 correct files. Of the 17 alarm cases, 8 are actual faults. **If** a file is selected with equal probability from these 100 files, then $P(\mathrm{fault}\mid\mathrm{alarm})=\frac{8}{17}\approx 47.1\,\%$; reversing the condition gives $P(\mathrm{alarm}\mid\mathrm{fault})=\frac{8}{10}=80\,\%$. Without the assumption of equal probabilities, these fractions are initially only **relative frequencies**, not universally valid model probabilities. The distinction prevents confusion between the detection rate and how informative an alarm is.

**Limit:** The modelled frequencies and probabilities must fit the real population. A statistical test result is neither a guarantee about the future nor automatically the probability of a hypothesis.

## How do I choose the right area? {#S7}

First ask the subject-specific question:

- **Change or optimum?** Derivatives, curve sketching and optimisation (calculus).
- **Accumulated quantity from a rate of change?** Definite integral (calculus).
- **Position, angle or collision?** Vectors, lines and planes (geometry).
- **Uncertain success counts and significance?** Distributions and statistical tests (probability and statistics).

In practice, several are often needed at the same time. A robot has a spatial position (**geometry**), moves with a velocity (**calculus**) and processes imprecise sensor data (**probability and statistics**).

## Requirements and limits of the overview {#S9}

This comparison is an **orientation sheet**, not a general calculation procedure. The derivative $f'(x)$ is only defined where the difference quotient has a finite limit. The antiderivative formula for a definite integral is applied here to a function that is continuous on the closed interval. For a discontinuous rate of change, the particular concept of integration and its requirements must be examined separately.

In geometry, calculating the angle from the scalar product requires **two non-zero vectors** in a suitably defined inner product space. Ordinary Euclidean length is not automatically the same as any arbitrary norm. In probability and statistics, $P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$ holds only when $P(B)>0$; in particular, $P(A\mid B)$ is generally **not** equal to $P(B\mid A)$. Observed proportions are not universally valid probabilities without a suitable sampling and probability model. Different software models can have different assumptions despite using identical formula symbols.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

A mathematical toolkit emerges for software and AI: functions and derivatives explain error gradients and control curves, vectors and matrices explain position and embedding relationships, and statistics explains uncertainty in measurement and model results. The real skill lies in recognising the appropriate question in a technical problem and being able to read the formula together with its assumptions.

## Connection to university {#S11}

School mathematics uses many familiar rules. University mathematics also asks **what the concepts mean precisely, under which conditions a statement holds and why it follows logically**. That is why university mathematics [M1-L1](?course=university_mathematics&module=M1&lesson=L1&syslang=en&doclang=en&theme=light) begins with sets, statements, quantifiers and principles of proof; university mathematics [M1-L2](?course=university_mathematics&module=M1&lesson=L2&syslang=en&doclang=en&theme=light) then shows how precisely the familiar idea of the limit can be expressed.

# M2-L9 – Fundamental theorem of calculus, growth and accumulated quantities {#L9}

**Course area:** M2 – Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Level:** Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Prior knowledge:** No specific prerequisites stated.

**Classification:** Upper secondary education at a Gymnasium · Integral calculus and modelling

## What is this about? {#S1}

Differential calculus answers the question of **how quickly a quantity is changing at that instant**. Integral calculus answers the question of **how these changes accumulate over a period of time**. The two methods are so closely connected that, under suitable conditions, they are in a sense reverse processes. This is expressed by the fundamental theorem of calculus. It enables many integrals to be calculated using antiderivatives and forms the basis of models of motion, growth, decay and accumulated quantities.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

If we have a curve representing an accumulated quantity, we can examine its **gradient**: this is its instantaneous rate of change. Conversely, if we know only the rate of change, we can **sum all the small changes** to determine the difference between the beginning and the end. The initial quantity is additional information: the same rate of change could correspond to very different curves of absolute quantities.

Example: A machine processes files sometimes faster and sometimes more slowly. The **processing rate** is not the **number of files already processed**. From the rate over time we can determine the increase in the number. How many files had been completed beforehand must be known separately.

## New symbols before the first formula {#S3}

| Symbol | Read aloud | Meaning here | Other possible uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| $G(x)$ | ‘capital gee of x’ | Accumulated change from a fixed lower limit to x | Any other function |
| $G'(x)$ | ‘capital gee prime of x’ | Instantaneous change in this accumulated quantity | Derivative notation from [M2-L3](M2-L3.md) |
| $\int_a^x$ | ‘integral from a to x’ | Fixed lower limit, variable upper limit | Limits may also be numbers or other variables |

| $e$ | ‘Euler’s number’, ‘ee’ | Base of the natural exponential function from [M1-L19](M1-L19.md) | Other constants in other subjects |

| $\approx$ | ‘is approximately equal to’ | Rounded numerical value | Approximation relation in general |

## The first connection: derivative of the accumulated change {#S4}

Suppose a continuous function $f$ describes a rate over time. For each endpoint $x$, we consider the accumulated change since a fixed starting point $a$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
G(x)&=\int_a^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
G^{\prime}(x)&=f(x)
\end{aligned}
$$

**First line read aloud:** ‘Capital gee of x is the integral of lower-case eff of tee with respect to tee, from a to x.’

**Second line read aloud:** ‘The derivative of capital gee at x equals lower-case eff of x.’

Here $t$ is simply a **variable of integration or running variable**. It identifies the points over which the contributions are summed. $x$ is the **variable upper limit**; the function $G$ therefore specifies how much has changed in total from the fixed limit $a$ to the current point $x$.

Intuitively, if we extend the time interval under consideration by a small amount, the additional change is approximately **rate at the end times additional time**. That is why the instantaneous rate of change of the accumulated quantity is the original function again. We assume here that $f$ is continuous on the interval under consideration. The equation $G'(x)=f(x)$ holds at **interior points**; at the endpoints of the interval we refer to one-sided derivatives where appropriate. Continuity at the point in question is the sufficient condition used here.

## The second connection: an antiderivative at two limits {#S5}

If you know an antiderivative $F$ of $f$, you can calculate the total change from two function values:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=F(b)-F(a)
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The integral of lower-case eff of x with respect to x from a to b equals capital eff at b minus capital eff at a.’

Why is this a reverse process? Because the derivative of $F$ equals $f$. Provided that $f$ is continuous on the interval between the limits and $F$ is an antiderivative there, the sum of all the small changes equals the **difference between the corresponding accumulated quantities**. As in [M2-L8](M2-L8.md), reversing the limits changes the sign. This equation was already used as a calculation rule in [M2-L8](M2-L8.md); [M2-L9](M2-L9.md) identifies it as part of the fundamental theorem.

## Fully explained mini-example: distance from velocity {#S6}

At $t=0$, a mobile device has an initial position of 40 kilometres on an imaginary axis along a path. Its velocity increases with time in a simplified model: $v(t)=6t$. Time $t$ is given in **hours** and velocity $v(t)$ in **kilometres per hour**; the numerical factor 6 therefore has the unit kilometres per hour squared. How far has the device moved forward after two hours?

The antiderivative of the **numerical-value function** $6t$ is $3t^2$. The factor $6$ in this model represents a constant acceleration of **$6\,\mathrm{km/h}^2$**, because time is substituted as a numerical value in hours. The product gives a velocity in km/h; after integration over hours, the result is a length in kilometres again. The initial position of 40 km is a **coordinate** on an imaginary axis, not a distance already travelled. We consider the change from 0 to 2 hours:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v(t)&=6t\\
\int_0^2 6t\,\mathrm{d}t&=\left[3t^2\right]_0^2\\
&=3\cdot2^2-3\cdot0^2\\
&=12\\
s(2)&=s_0+12=40+12=52
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The integral of six times tee with respect to tee from zero to two is twelve; the position after two hours is the initial position forty plus twelve, or fifty-two kilometres.’

The square brackets `[3t²] from 0 to 2` are an **evaluation instruction** here: first substitute the upper limit, then subtract the value at the lower limit. Elsewhere, square brackets can denote intervals, vectors or lists.

Important: The twelve kilometres represent **displacement**, not automatically the total distance travelled if direction changes. In our model the velocity is non-negative during the period considered, so the two are equal here.

## A model of an accumulated quantity as a general principle {#S7}

A quantity $B$ (pronounced ‘capital bee’) describes an **accumulated quantity**, such as an amount of data, a volume of water or the number of entries already processed. The initial quantity is called $B_0$ (‘bee nought’). We call a time-dependent rate of change $r(t)$ (‘ar of tee’).

$$
B(t)=B_0+\int_0^t r(u)\,\mathrm{d}u
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The accumulated quantity bee at time tee is the initial quantity bee nought plus the integral of the rate ar of u with respect to u from zero to tee.’

$u$ (pronounced ‘you’) is simply a **running variable** within the integral; we do not use $t$ in the integrand because $t$ already represents the upper limit here. $r(u)$ may increase, decrease or take negative values. A positive rate increases the quantity, and a negative rate reduces it.

**Technical application:** The difference between the number of messages arriving and being processed per second can be modelled as the net rate of change of the queue length. This continuous model is an approximation: real messages arrive discretely, and the number of waiting messages cannot have arbitrary fractional values. A calculated negative quantity would be impossible in a real queue; in that case, the processing rate, empty state or model equation, for instance, must be adjusted.

## Growth and decay: what distinguishes two types of model? {#S8}

With **linear growth**, the quantity increases by a constant absolute amount per unit of time. With **exponential growth**, the instantaneous change is proportional to the quantity currently present. We know the constant of proportionality $k$ from the previous lessons; in time models it often has the unit ‘per hour’ or ‘per day’.

$$
\begin{aligned}
B^{\prime}(t)&=kB(t)\\
B(t)&=B_0e^{kt}
\end{aligned}
$$

**First formula read aloud:** ‘The derivative of the accumulated quantity bee with respect to tee equals kay times the current quantity bee of tee.’

**Second formula read aloud:** ‘The accumulated quantity bee after tee equals the initial quantity bee nought times e to the power kay times tee.’

For positive $k$, there is growth when $B_0>0$; for negative $k$, there is decay. In a time model, $k$ has the unit **1/time**; this makes $k\cdot t$ in the exponent dimensionless. The notation $e^{kt}$ means that **the entire product $k\cdot t$ is in the exponent**. For $k=0$, the quantity remains constant; $B_0=0$ also gives the zero function for every $k$. **Checking by differentiation:** The derivative of $B_0\cdot e^{kt}$ with respect to $t$ is $k\cdot B_0\cdot e^{kt}=k\cdot B(t)$. This function therefore satisfies the model equation and the initial value $B(0)=B_0$.

**Brief interpretation:** A **decay parameter** $k=-0.2$ per day does not mean a linear decrease of 20 percentage points each day: the **instantaneous relative rate of decrease** is $-0.2$ per day. After **one whole day**, the remaining factor in the continuous model is $e^{-0.2}\approx0.8187$, or about **81.87%** of the initial quantity. This is a decrease of approximately **18.13%** during that day, not exactly 20%. The absolute decrease over each equally long time interval becomes smaller as the quantity falls.

## Three perspectives on the same quantity {#S9}

| Information | Mathematical question | Typical tool |
|---|---|---|
| Accumulated quantity $B(t)$ | ‘How much is present at time t?’ | Function value |
| Rate of change $B'(t)$ | ‘How quickly is the quantity growing or decreasing at this instant?’ | Derivative |
| Change between two instants | ‘How much has been added or lost in total?’ | Definite integral |

The **units** must match: kilometres per hour multiplied by hours gives kilometres; megabytes per second multiplied by seconds gives megabytes. Checking the units is often the quickest way to identify a misinterpreted formula.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

The connection between the derivative and the integral is central to robotics and motion control: position, velocity and acceleration are related in this way. In data analysis, rates can be summed to obtain total quantities. In AI and simulation models, differential equations describe how a state changes continuously; the solution reconstructs the evolution of the state from initial conditions.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

- For the simple form of the fundamental theorem used here, we consider in particular **continuous rates of change** on the relevant interval. University-level study broadens these conditions.
- The fundamental theorem is no substitute for suitable modelling assumptions. If real events are discrete or discontinuous, a continuous model may be only an approximation.
- A rate alone does **not** determine the initial quantity; an additional initial condition is needed for that.
- Integrating **signed** velocity gives displacement; for the total distance travelled when direction changes, the magnitude of velocity must be integrated.
- Exponential models assume a constant relative rate of change within the model. In real systems, capacity limits and interventions often lead to different behaviour. A positive initial quantity remains positive in this model; real quantities may also be discrete and bounded.

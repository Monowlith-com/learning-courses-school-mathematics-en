# M1-L3 – Laws of arithmetic, brackets, expressions and equivalent transformations {#L3}

**Course area:** M1 – Lower secondary education (Sekundarstufe I)

**Level:** Lower secondary education

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Lower secondary education, roughly Year 7 of the German school system · Format: quick refresher

## What is this about? {#S1}

A mathematical expression can be written in different ways and still have the same value. The laws of arithmetic allow us to simplify it. Under certain rules, we can rearrange equations without changing their solutions. These foundations help us later to read larger formulas and understand algorithms or models correctly.

## Commutative law – changing the order {#S2}

In **addition** and **multiplication** of ordinary numbers, we can swap the order: $3+5=5+3$ and $2\cdot4=4\cdot2$.

**Read aloud:** ‘Three plus five equals five plus three’ and ‘two times four equals four times two’ respectively.

We **cannot generally** swap the order in ordinary subtraction and division: $5-3$ is different from $3-5$. With matrices, even swapping the order of multiplication is often not allowed.

## Associative law – changing the grouping {#S3}

In addition or multiplication we can change the grouping: $(2+3)+4=2+(3+4)$.

**Read aloud:** ‘The sum of two and three, plus four, equals two plus the sum of three and four.’ Both sides give 9. On computers, rounding may cause tiny differences when using floating-point numbers.

## Distributive law – expanding brackets {#S4}

The letters $a$, $b$, $c$ (‘ay’, ‘bee’, ‘see’) stand for numbers here. A letter immediately before a bracket means **multiplication**. $ab$ means $a\cdot b$.

**Formula:** $a(b+c)=ab+ac$

**Read aloud:** ‘a times the sum of b and c equals a times b plus a times c.’

**Mini-example:** $3(4+2)=3\cdot4+3\cdot2=18$. Read in reverse, the rule allows us to **take out common factors** or **factorise**.

## Expressions and equations {#S5}

An **expression** is a mathematical expression, for example $3x+5$ (‘three times x plus five’). An **equation** is formed only when we set it equal to a second expression: $3x+5=20$.

An expression can take different values depending on which permissible numerical value we substitute for $x$.

## Equivalent transformations – keeping the scales balanced {#S6}

Imagine the sides of an equation as two pans of balanced scales. If we add or subtract the same number on both sides, the equality remains. We may also multiply or divide both sides by the same **non-zero** number.

**Example:** $3(x+2)=21$.

**Read aloud:** ‘Three times the sum of x and two equals twenty-one.’

Divide both sides by 3: $x+2=7$. Then subtract 2 from both sides: $x=5$. Check: $3\cdot(5+2)=21$.

The symbol `⇔` (‘is equivalent to’ or ‘if and only if’) connects two logically equivalent statements. In equations, it shows that both have the same **solution set**, for example $x+2=7\iff x=5$.

## Order of operations {#S7}

Usually brackets come first, followed by powers and roots, then multiplication and division, and finally addition and subtraction. **Addition and subtraction, and multiplication and division** are usually evaluated from left to right when they have equal priority. **This is not a general rule for successive powers:** the expressions ${(2^3)}^2=64$ (‘two cubed, then squared, equals sixty-four’) and $2^{3^2}=512$ (‘two to the power of three squared equals five hundred and twelve’) are different. Therefore, use explicit brackets to group nested powers; programming languages may have their own associativity rules.

| Expression | Result | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| $(2+3)\cdot4$ | 20 | Brackets first |
| $3+4\cdot2$ | 11 | Multiplication before addition |
| $12-3+2$ | 11 | Equal priority, from left to right |
| $2^3$ | 8 | Power: two cubed = $2\cdot2\cdot2$ |

## Guide to reading symbols {#S8}

| Symbol | Pronunciation | Meaning here | Other possible uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| $a,b,c,x$ | ay, bee, see, ex | Numbers or variables | Parameters, coordinates |
| ( ) | open / close bracket | Grouped calculation | Function argument, tuple |
| = | equals | Same value | Sometimes assignment in code |
| ⇔ | is equivalent to | Same solution set | Logical ‘if and only if’ |
| ≠ | is not equal to | Different values | Comparison condition |
| $2^3$ | two cubed | Power | Generally $a^n$ |

## What is it useful for? {#S9}

Parsers for formulas or programming languages must interpret brackets and operator priorities correctly. In data processing, algebraic transformations allow more efficient expressions; in modelling, an unknown quantity can be isolated. Take care with computer numbers: rearrangements may give slightly different results because of rounding.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S10}

Dividing by an expression that could be zero may lose solutions or result in meaningless operations. Squaring or taking a root may require additional conditions; not every seemingly plausible rearrangement is an **equivalent transformation**.

# M2-L1 – Families of functions in calculus {#L1}

**Course area:** M2 – Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Level:** Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Prior knowledge:** No specific prerequisites stated.

**Classification:** Upper secondary education at a Gymnasium, calculus / foundations for the basic and advanced courses (Grundkurs and Leistungskurs) · Format: systematic overview with guidance on reading notation

## What is this about? {#S1}

In calculus we study not just individual numbers, but entire **relationships between quantities**. A function takes an admissible input and assigns exactly one value to it. Different types of functions produce different patterns of change: straight-line, curved, exponentially growing or periodically oscillating patterns. If we recognise the family of functions, we often already know which questions make sense — for example, about growth, limits, gradients, extreme values or long-term behaviour. The next lessons take us from recognising these shapes to **limits and derivatives**.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine a computer program that supplies measurements as a function of time. A constant processing rate leads to a **straight line**; accelerating change may be approximated by a **parabola**; a fixed percentage increase per step leads to **exponential growth**; a regularly repeating signal may be represented by a **sine function**. Mathematically, the basic idea is the same: each admissible input has exactly one output. The different families of functions differ in *how* that value is calculated.

## Polynomials: sums of powers {#S4}

A **polynomial** is a finite sum of multiples of non-negative integer powers of a variable. Straight lines and parabolas both belong to this family.

$$
f(x)=a\cdot x^2+b\cdot x+c
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Eff of x equals a times x squared plus b times x plus c.’ The parameters $a$, $b$ and $c$ are fixed numbers; $x$ is the input; $x^2$ means $x$ times $x$. When $a\ne0$, we obtain a **quadratic polynomial** — the familiar parabola from M1-L13. If $a=0$ and $b\ne0$, it is a polynomial of degree one. If $a=0$ and $b=0$, the constant function $f(x)=c$ remains (degree zero for $c\ne0$; the zero polynomial is treated separately).

Polynomials can have several extreme points or roots. Among other things, they are suitable for locally approximating smooth characteristic curves in engineering. Whether an approximation still makes sense outside the range of the data must be checked separately.

## Rational functions: one polynomial divided by another {#S5}

A **rational** expression has a polynomial in the numerator and a polynomial in the denominator. The most important additional question is: **When is the denominator zero?** These inputs are excluded.

$$
g(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-2}\qquad\text{only for }x\neq2
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Gee of x equals x plus one, divided by x minus two; x must not be two.’ $g$ names the function; $(x+1)$ is the numerator, $(x-2)$ the denominator, and `/` means divided by. At $x=2$ the denominator would be zero — the function is **undefined** there. As $x$ approaches two, the magnitude of the function value can become very large; we return to this behaviour in M2-L2.

Such quotients arise, for example, in ratios such as cost per unit, speed calculated from distance and time, or rational model functions. A zero denominator is a mathematical problem; it must not simply be replaced with an arbitrarily invented number.

## Exponential functions: repeated growth by the same factor {#S6}

In an exponential function the **variable is in the exponent**, while the base is fixed.

$$
f(x)=2^x
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Eff of x equals two to the power of x.’ $2$ is the fixed base; $x$ determines the exponent. For example, the value at $x=0$ is one, at $x=1$ it is two, and at $x=3$ it is eight. An exponential function with a base greater than one describes growth, provided that $x$ is considered in equal steps.

More generally, a non-constant real exponential function $f(x)=a^x$ is defined for $a>0$ with $a\ne1$ and takes **only positive function values**. A base between zero and one gives exponential decay as $x$ increases. The base $a=1$ would give only the constant function $f(x)=1$. The frequently used **Euler’s number e** is a special base; $e^x$ has properties that are particularly convenient in differential calculus.

## Logarithmic functions: finding the exponent {#S7}

The logarithmic function reverses an exponential function. It answers the question: **What exponent do I need to obtain a particular number?**

$$
\log_2(8)=3\qquad\text{because }2^3=8
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The logarithm of eight to base two equals three, because two cubed equals eight.’ `₂` denotes the base two, not multiplication by two. $\log_2$ is the name of the corresponding logarithmic function.

The **natural logarithm** $\ln(x)$ uses the base $e$, Euler’s number. As a real function, $\ln(x)$ is defined only for positive $x$. Logarithms arise, among other things, in the study of growth processes, orders of magnitude and measures of information.

## Trigonometric functions: repeating patterns {#S8}

The **sine** and **cosine functions** assign coordinates on the unit circle to an angle ([M1-L20](M1-L20.md)). As functions of a real number, they can also model repeated oscillations.

$$
f(x)=\sin(x)
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Eff of x equals sine of x.’ Here $x$ generally denotes an angle in **radians**, not automatically in degrees. At $x=0$ the function value is zero; at $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ it is one; after a full period of $2\pi$ the pattern repeats.

Such periodic functions are central to **audio engineering, waves, sensors, rotation and signal processing**. A real signal, however, generally does not consist of exactly one sine wave, but may combine several components and disturbances.

## Comparison of the most important families of functions {#S9}

| Family of functions | Typical notation | Characteristics | Typical restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear / polynomial of degree one | $f(x)=mx+b$, $m\ne0$ | Equal change per unit of x | When $m=0$, only a constant function; limit extrapolation |
| Quadratic / polynomial of degree two | $f(x)=ax^2+bx+c$, $a\ne0$ | Parabola, curvature, possible maximum/minimum | When $a=0$, not quadratic; consider model domain |
| Rational | $f(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ | Quotient of polynomials | $q(x)\ne0$ |
| Exponential | $f(x)=a^x$ | Same multiplication factor per step of x | $a>0$, and for a non-constant model $a\ne1$ |
| Logarithmic | $f(x)=\ln(x)$ | Inverse of an exponential function | $x>0$ |
| Trigonometric | $f(x)=\sin(x)$ | Recurring, periodic pattern | Consider angular units and actual signal shape |

The names $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ in the row for rational functions refer to **polynomials**, not probabilities; $m$ and $b$ are the parameters of a straight line familiar from M1-L8. These examples do not specify every possible form in each family.

## Fully explained mini-example: linear or exponential? {#S10}

A service initially processes **100 tasks**. Two simple models are possible: it increases its performance in each time interval by **20 additional tasks** or by **20 per cent of the current amount**.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Linear:}\quad L(t)&=100+20t\\
\text{Exponential:}\quad G(t)&=100\cdot\left(1.2\right)^t
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Ell of t equals one hundred plus twenty times t.’ And: ‘Gee of t equals one hundred times one point two to the power of t.’ $t$ is the number of equal-length time steps ($t=0,1,2,3,\ldots$ in the discrete model considered here); $L$ and $G$ are model names; $1.2$ is the growth factor for an increase of 20 per cent per step. Intermediate values for non-integer $t$ would be an additional modelling assumption.

| Time step $t$ | Linear $L(t)$ | Exponential $G(t)$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100 | 100 |
| 1 | 120 | 120 |
| 2 | 140 | 144 |
| 5 | 200 | $248.832$ |

The two models initially give similar values but later develop differently. The mathematically more appropriate model follows **not from the prettier graph**, but from the real mechanism: are a fixed 20 tasks added per step, or does the number grow proportionally to the amount already reached? Actual server capacity cannot grow exponentially without limit either; the assumption can apply only within a certain range.

## What does calculus study about functions? {#S11}

With the families of functions we already know, we can ask: Which inputs are allowed? What is the function value for a particular input? Where does the graph cross the axes? What pattern of change can we recognise?

**Calculus** adds further questions: **What value does a function approach?** How quickly does it change **at one particular instant**? Where does it reach a maximum or minimum value? And how much has **accumulated** over a period of time? This gives rise to the concepts of *limit*, *derivative* and *integral* — introduced in detail only in the following lessons.

## What is it useful for? {#S12}

In **software engineering**, choosing a family of functions is part of the modelling problem: runtimes, capacities and costs can follow linear, curved or exponential patterns. In **AI**, function shapes are used in activation functions, probability models and optimisation objectives. For **measurements and signals**, periodic models make it possible to distinguish regular processes from irregular deviations.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S13}

Assigning a function to a family is initially a **mathematical classification**, not empirical proof that a real system works that way. Domains must always be considered: division by zero is not allowed, real logarithms require positive arguments, and trigonometric formulas require an unambiguous angular unit. With discrete data, a continuous curve may also be merely a modelling assumption.

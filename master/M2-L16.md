# M2-L16 – Random variables, expected value, spread and tests {#L16}

**Course area:** M2 – Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Level:** Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Upper secondary education (Gymnasium) · probability and statistics; supplementary advanced material is marked.

## What is this about? {#S1}

After [M2-L15](M2-L15.md), we can calculate how likely a particular number of successes is. Now we want to summarise entire probability distributions: what value can we expect on average? How much do the results vary? And how can we check whether observed data are consistent with a particular assumption? These questions lead to expected value, variance, standard deviation, the normal distribution and statistical tests. It remains important that statistics gives conclusions **under modelling assumptions**, not certainty about an individual future case.

## Basic idea and visual intuition {#S2}

Imagine repeating the same type of experiment very often. The observed numbers vary, but show characteristic patterns in their frequency distribution. The **expected value** describes the mathematical centre of a probability distribution; the **variance** and **standard deviation** describe the spread around this centre. A **normal distribution** is a continuous, bell-shaped model for some measurement and summation effects. Finally, with a **hypothesis test**, we ask how unusual data would be **if a particular assumption about how they arose were true**.

The summation sign in this chapter means: for every permitted possibility we calculate the relevant value and then add all the contributions. A statistical quantity may describe a **sample mean** from observed data or a **theoretical expected value** of a model. These are different quantities, even if the numbers look similar.

## Random variables: translating outcomes into numbers {#S4}

A random variable does not itself have to 'be random' like an uncontrolled variable in a program. It is a **specified mapping**: a number is assigned to every possible outcome of the random experiment according to a clear rule.

**Mini-example:** We toss two independent, fair coins and count how often heads appears. The random variable $X$ can take the values zero, one and two. Its probability table is:

| $X$ = number of heads | $P(X=x)$ | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $\frac14$ | tails–tails |
| 1 | $\frac12$ | heads–tails or tails–heads |
| 2 | $\frac14$ | heads–heads |

The individual probabilities add up to one. This table is called a **discrete probability distribution**: we can list all possible values.

## Expected value: where is the mathematical centre? {#S5}

For a discrete random variable, we calculate the expected value by multiplying every possible numerical value by its probability and adding the products.

$$
E[X]=\sum_i\bigl(x_i\cdot P(X=x_i)\bigr)
$$

**Read aloud in full:** 'The expected value of X is the sum over all possible values x i, where each value x i is multiplied by the probability that X takes exactly that value.' Capital sigma is therefore an instruction to add many weighted contributions.

For the two coins we obtain:

$$
E[X]=0\cdot\frac14+1\cdot\frac12+2\cdot\frac14=1
$$

**Read aloud:** 'Zero times one quarter plus one times one half plus two times one quarter equals one.' The expected value is **one head per two tosses**. This does not mean that exactly one head occurs in every pair of tosses. The expected value is an average over the theoretical model, or over many independent repetitions in the long run under suitable conditions.

For a binomially distributed random variable with $n$ trials and probability of success $p$, the result is particularly simple: $E[X]=n\cdot p$. **Read aloud:** 'The expected number of successes is the number of trials times the probability of success.' The symbols $n$ and $p$ were explained in M2-L15. In that example ($n=3$, $p=0.2$), the theoretical expected value is $3\cdot0.2=0.6$ successes; individual results are still whole numbers. **Do not confuse:** A sample mean is calculated from actual measured values, whereas $E[X]$ comes from a specified probability model.

## Variance and standard deviation: how much does X vary? {#S6}

Two distributions can have the same expected value but very different spreads. A mean alone does not show this. We therefore calculate the mean **squared deviation** of each value from the expected value $\mu$.

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_i\bigl[{(x_i-\mu)}^2\cdot P(X=x_i)\bigr]
$$

**Read aloud in full:** 'The variance of X is the sum of the squared distances of the possible values x i from the mean mu, each multiplied by the probability of that value.' Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from simply cancelling when added. At first we also obtain a **squared unit**, for example seconds squared.

The **standard deviation** brings the spread back to the unit of the original quantity:

$$
\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}
$$

**Read aloud:** 'Sigma is the square root of the variance of X.' Sigma is non-negative. In this model, a small standard deviation indicates more concentrated values, and a large one indicates more widely spread values.

### Fully explained mini-example {#abschnitt-7}

Our two-coin distribution has mean $\mu =1$. Therefore the deviations for the values zero, one and two are minus one, zero and plus one, respectively. Squaring gives one, zero and one.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)&=1\cdot\frac14+0\cdot\frac12+1\cdot\frac14=\frac12\\
\sigma&=\sqrt{\frac12}\approx0.7071
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** 'The variance is one half; the standard deviation is the square root of one half, or approximately zero point seven zero seven one.' The mathematical spread is meaningful even though only whole numbers of successes can occur in an individual trial.

For a binomial distribution, the special formula is $\operatorname{Var}(X)=n\cdot p\cdot(1-p)$. **Read aloud:** 'The variance is n times p times one minus p.' It applies to the independent Bernoulli trials with the same probability of success in the binomial model, not to just any sequence of tests. For $n=3$ and $p=0.2$, we have $\operatorname{Var}(X)=3\cdot0.2\cdot0.8=0.48$ and the standard deviation $\sqrt{0.48}\approx0.693$. This is the **theoretical variance of the distribution**, not automatically the variance estimated from a small sample; different formulae are used for sample estimation.

## Normal distribution: the bell curve {#S7}

The normal distribution is a **continuous** model. Most values lie near its mean, and extremely distant values become increasingly rare towards the edges. Its shape is described by $\mu$ and $\sigma$. The notation $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is read as 'X is normally distributed with mean mu and variance sigma squared'. The symbol $\sim$ means 'is distributed as' **in this particular notation**, not the geometrical similarity from M1-L11.

Values are often standardised:

$$
z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
$$

**Read aloud:** 'The standardised value z is the observed value x minus the mean mu, divided by the standard deviation sigma.' A $z$ of two means that the value in question lies two standard deviations above the mean. The requirement is $\sigma >0$.

**Example:** A measurement of 110 with a modelled mean of 100 and standard deviation of five corresponds to $z=\frac{110-100}{5}=2$.

With the normal distribution, probabilities are determined for **intervals**, not for a single exact point. The area under the density curve over an interval gives the probability of that interval. The total area is one. For an ideal continuous normally distributed random variable, any individual exact outcome has probability **zero**, even though intervals can have positive probabilities. When $\sigma =0$, there is no longer a normal distribution with a bell-shaped density. Not every real series of measurements is normally distributed; strong asymmetry, dependence, a few discrete values and outliers may call for other models.

**Further study / outlook:** For sufficiently large $n$ and values of $p$ that are not too extreme, a binomial distribution can be approximated by a normal distribution. For small or very skewed samples this should not be done uncritically; exact binomial calculation remains a natural choice.

## Hypothesis tests: what do data tell us about an assumption? {#S8}

A statistical test begins with **two competing statements**:

- $H_0$ (*null hypothesis*): A particular assumption about the model holds.
- $H_1$ (*alternative hypothesis*): The departure from that assumption under investigation holds.

We decide in advance which data should count as particularly inconsistent with $H_0$. We also set the **significance level** $\alpha$, often five per cent, for example. We then calculate a **p-value** from the data, which indicates how probable a result at least as extreme would be **under the null hypothesis**.

### Mini-example with a test that can be checked {#abschnitt-10}

Under the null assumption, an automated procedure makes the correct decision with probability $0.5$ on each independent trial. It is tested **20 times** and produces **15 correct results**. We carry out a *one-sided* test of whether the probability of success is greater than 0.5:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_0&:\ \text{probability of success }p=0.5\\
H_1&:\ \text{probability of success }p>0.5\\
n&=20,\quad\text{observed }X=15
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** 'Under H zero, the probability of success is one half; under H one, it is greater than one half. In twenty trials, fifteen successes are observed.' Under $H_0$, we can use the binomial distribution from [M2-L15](M2-L15.md) and add the probabilities of **15 or more** successes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{p-value}&=P(X\ge15\mid H_0)\\
&=\frac{\binom{20}{15}+\binom{20}{16}+\binom{20}{17}+\binom{20}{18}+\binom{20}{19}+\binom{20}{20}}{2^{20}}\\
&=\frac{21\,700}{1\,048\,576}\\
&\approx 0.0207=2.07\,\%
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** 'Under the null hypothesis, the probability of at least fifteen successes in twenty trials is approximately two point zero seven per cent.' If $\alpha =0.05$ was set **before** the test, the p-value is below this threshold. In this test we then reject $H_0$ in favour of $H_1$. To check the **one-sided** decision: with only 14 successes the upper binomial tail would be approximately $0.0577$, and thus **above** 5%. The exact rejection region for this test is therefore $X\geq 15$. If we instead used a two-sided test for a departure upwards **or** downwards, the p-value would be different; the test question must therefore be decided before the calculation.

This does **not** mean that the null hypothesis is true with a probability of just 2.07 per cent. Nor does it prove that the procedure will perform better on all future data. Without random and representative samples or when trials are dependent, the result can be misleading.

## Statistical uncertainty and typical misconceptions {#S9}

A hypothesis test is a decision procedure with possible errors. A **type I error** occurs when we reject a null hypothesis that actually holds. Under the test assumptions, the significance level set in advance limits the **probability** of this error over repeated similar tests to at most $\alpha$. Because the number of successes is discrete, the actual error probability in the above test at the null value $p=0.5$ is only about 2.07%, even though $\alpha=5\,\%$ was specified. A **type II error** occurs when we fail to reject a false null hypothesis; its probability depends, among other things, on the actual effect and sample size.

Likewise, **not significant** does not automatically mean 'no effect'. And **significant** does not automatically mean 'large or important effect'. Additional information comes, for example, from effect sizes, appropriate uncertainty intervals and a review of the data collection. If many tests are tried and only the most striking ones are published, the risk of misleading findings increases.

**Further study:** Confidence intervals, statistical power, multiple tests and Bayesian statistics are useful topics for further study. In the usual frequentist interpretation, a 95 per cent confidence interval does not mean a 95 per cent probability that an already fixed parameter lies in this particular calculated interval. It describes the long-run coverage rate of the procedure under the assumptions made.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

In software diagnostics, the standard deviation summarises the variation in latencies or measurement errors, though often only after examining the shape of the distribution. In AI benchmarks, expected values and uncertainties are useful for looking beyond a single success rate. Quality control uses statistical tests to distinguish systematic changes from random fluctuations. In each of these cases, the measurement setup, independence and data selection must fit the model.

## Assumptions and limits {#S11}

Expected value and variance require the corresponding sums or integrals to exist in the model used. A theoretical distribution model is not identical to an empirical sample. For tests, the question, direction of the alternative, choice of model and significance level should ideally be determined **before** inspecting the results. A p-value is not a measure of the probability of a hypothesis and is no substitute for subject knowledge or reproducibility.

# M2-L8 – Definite integrals and signed area {#L8}

**Course area:** M2 – Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Level:** Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Prior knowledge:** No specific prerequisites stated.

**Classification:** Upper secondary education at a Gymnasium · Integral calculus

## What is this about? {#S1}

A rate of change tells us how quickly something changes **at a particular instant**. Often, however, we are interested in the **total change over a period of time**. For example, we might know how many parcels a machine processes per hour and ask how many were processed during an entire shift. The definite integral combines very many small contributions into an overall net change. Unlike the indefinite integral from [M2-L7](M2-L7.md), it generally gives **a numerical value** for fixed limits, not a whole family of antiderivatives.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine a curve showing the rate of inflow or outflow at each instant. If we split time into small intervals, we can approximate each interval by a rectangle: **rate times duration** gives a contribution to the stored quantity. Many such contributions give an approximation to the total change. As the time intervals become finer, a limit is obtained under suitable conditions: the definite integral.

![Figure: The definite integral adds signed areas: $-0.5+2=1.5$ over the interval $[0,3]$.](../media/img/S_MAT_027_EN.svg){alt="Straight line f(x) = x − 1 on the interval from 0 to 3. Red area −0.5 below the x-axis; green area +2 above it; signed total +1.5."}

Original illustration of the signed area for $f(x)=x-1$ on \[0,3\]: $-0.5+2=1.5$.

Graphically, this looks like an area between the curve and the horizontal axis. The crucial point, however, is the sign: above the axis the area counts as **positive**, below it as **negative**. The result is therefore called the *oriented area* or *signed area*.

## New symbols before the first formula {#S3}

| Symbol | Read aloud | Meaning here | Other possible uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\int$ | ‘integral of’ | Summing contributions distributed over infinitely fine intervals; already introduced in [M2-L7](M2-L7.md) | Without limits: family of antiderivatives |
| $a$ | ‘ay’ | Lower limit of integration / starting point | General number or parameter |
| $b$ | ‘bee’ | Upper limit of integration / endpoint | Function parameter or other number |
| $\int_a^b$ | ‘integral from a to b’ | Definite integral with a specified interval | In LaTeX: `int_a^b` |
| $f(x)$ | ‘eff of x’ | Function to be summed, e.g. a rate | General function rule |
| $\mathrm{d}x$ | ‘dee x’ | Integration is with respect to the variable x | Differential notation, made more precise later |
| $F(b)-F(a)$ | ‘capital eff of b minus capital eff of a’ | Difference between the values of an antiderivative at the limits | $F$ may otherwise be another function |
| $\sum$ | ‘capital sigma’, ‘sum’ | Sign for adding many contributions; introduced here in anticipation of later work | General summation sign in statistics and computer science |
| $\Delta x$ | ‘delta x’ | Width of a small interval | Change or distance between two x-values; familiar from [M1-L8](M1-L8.md) |

## An integral with upper and lower limits {#S4}

The **lower limit** is written at the bottom of the integral sign and the **upper limit** at the top. The integrand is written between the integral sign and the differential $\mathrm{d}x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The definite integral of lower-case eff of x with respect to x, from the lower limit a to the upper limit b.’

Here $f(x)$ represents the height or rate of change at the relevant point; $\mathrm{d}x$ identifies the variable and reminds us that contributions are summed along its axis. $a$ and $b$ delimit exactly the interval being considered. For a **continuous function on the closed interval** between $a$ and $b$, this integral (the Riemann integral) exists. Many functions with isolated jump discontinuities are also integrable; continuity is **sufficient but not always necessary**.

**Important difference from [M2-L7](M2-L7.md):** $\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ without limits denotes a family of antiderivatives with $+C$. $\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ with limits is a net numerical result – with the corresponding units in a correctly formulated mathematical model. No arbitrary constant of integration appears in the result of the definite integral.

## How calculation using an antiderivative works {#S5}

If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on the interval under consideration, then for a continuous $f$:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=F(b)-F(a)
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The definite integral of f of x with respect to x between a and b equals the value of the antiderivative capital F at the upper limit b minus its value at the lower limit a.’

The **roles of the symbols**: $\int$ sums the contributions; $a$, $b$ define the interval; $f(x)$ is the integrand; $\mathrm{d}x$ specifies the variable of integration; $F$ is any antiderivative with $F'(x)=f(x)$; `−` forms the difference between the end and the beginning. The constant $C$ disappears automatically because it would occur at both the end and the beginning and cancel out.

This calculation rule is part of the **fundamental theorem of calculus**, which [M2-L9](M2-L9.md) explains in more detail. Here we use it in practice first.

## Fully explained mini-example: positive and negative contributions {#S6}

Suppose a simplified rate of change is $f(t)=2t-2$. The variable $t$ stands for the **numerical value** of time in hours, and $f(t)$ for the **numerical value** of the net rate of change in items per hour. The number $2$ before $t$ belongs to the gradient, measured in items per hour squared; the constant $-2$ belongs to the rate, measured in items per hour. The formula is an equation between numerical values for these specified units. We consider the interval from 0 to 3 hours.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(t)&=2t-2\\
F(t)&=t^2-2t\\
\int_0^3(2t-2)\,\mathrm{d}t&=F(3)-F(0)\\
&=(9-6)-(0-0)\\
&=3
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The integral of two times tee minus two with respect to tee, from zero to three, is three.’ We have found an antiderivative, substituted the upper limit and then subtracted the value at the lower limit. The result represents a **net increase of three items** over three hours.

Why is this not simply the total geometrical area? Initially, $f(0)=-2$: the rate is negative. At $t=1$ it is zero, and afterwards it is positive. The negative contribution between 0 and 1 is **−1 item**; the positive contribution between 1 and 3 is **+4 items**. Together these give the net change **$-1+4=3\,\text{items}$**. By contrast, the accumulated **magnitude of the change** over the whole interval is $1+4=5\,\mathrm{items}$. In the appropriately scaled time–rate diagram, this corresponds to the sum of the **unsigned component areas**. The numerical result is the same, but its physical unit comes from ‘rate times time’, which here gives items.

**An important distinction:** A negative rate of change may mean a **decrease** in the stored quantity without the quantity itself being negative.

## Geometrical area and signed area are not the same {#S7}

| Question | What is counted? | Result in the example |
|---|---|---|
| ‘What is the **net change**?’ | Positive contributions minus the magnitudes of negative contributions | $3$ items |
| ‘What is the **sum of the non-negative component areas**?’ | All contributions counted positively, splitting at zeros if necessary | Numerical value $5$; in the rate model $5$ items |
| ‘How far has something **travelled in total**, regardless of direction?’ | In motion models, often the integral of the magnitude of velocity | Not the same as displacement |

For the geometrical area, we can integrate the **absolute value** of a suitable integrable function on a **finite interval**. The notation $|f(x)|$ means ‘absolute value of f of x’; the vertical bars turn negative function values into positive ones. This is the right answer only when **all parts of the area are to be counted positively**.

## Reversing the limits and splitting the interval {#S8}

Two practical rules are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=-\int_b^a f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\int_a^c f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_c^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
\end{aligned}
$$

**First rule read aloud:** ‘The integral from a to b is the negative of the integral from b to a.’ Reversing the direction reverses the sign.

**Second rule read aloud:** ‘The net change from a to c plus the net change from c to b gives the net change from a to b.’ The new intermediate point $c$ (pronounced ‘see’) splits the interval. In linear plain-text notation `∫_a^c`, the underscore identifies the lower limit and the caret the upper limit.

In the example, we can use this to examine the change of sign at $t=1$ separately. The intermediate point need not lie between a and b for algebraic additivity to hold; for an intuitive division of a time interval, we usually choose it within that interval.

## From rectangle approximations to the limit {#S9}

A rough calculation might use a representative rate for each time interval, multiply it by the duration of the interval and add all the contributions. For sufficiently regular functions, the approximation becomes more accurate as the rectangles become narrower. **Concrete check:** In our linear example, three rectangles of one hour each, using the **left-hand** endpoint value in each case, give the approximation $-2+0+2=0$ items; six rectangles of half an hour each give $1.5$ items. The exact net change is $3$ items. A finer division improves the left-hand approximation here; for other functions, it need not improve monotonically at every intermediate step. Mathematical notation often uses a capital sigma, the summation sign $\sum$, and a limiting process. We will study the complete construction of such *Riemann sums* later at university.

This is the bridge between a textbook formula and a practical numerical algorithm: computer programs can approximate the integral using discrete values even when no simple antiderivative is known.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

In computer science, the total data transmitted can be calculated from a data rate that varies over time. In a simulation, integrating signed velocity gives displacement; the magnitude may be needed to obtain the total distance travelled. In AI systems and sensors, integrals help summarise signals that vary over time. For cost or energy profiles, a rate per unit time gives a total quantity.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

- The result is a **net value**, not automatically a positive area.
- The limits, units and sign must match the real model. ‘Items per hour’ multiplied by ‘hours’ gives items.
- $F(b)-F(a)$ requires an appropriate antiderivative on the interval; if no simple closed-form expression exists, numerical methods are possible.
- When measurements are available only at individual points, assumptions about behaviour **between** the measurement points are needed. Integral calculus cannot determine a unique area from such points alone.
- If the function is not properly integrable on the entire interval, the case must be examined separately; in particular, unbounded functions or infinite intervals later lead to **improper integrals**.

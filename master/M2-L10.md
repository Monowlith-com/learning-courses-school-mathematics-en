# M2-L10 – Advanced calculus and selected advanced-course (Leistungskurs) topics {#L10}

**Course area:** M2 – Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Level:** Upper secondary education / Abitur

**Prior knowledge:** No specific prerequisites stated.

**Classification:** Upper secondary education at a Gymnasium · Advanced level / Leistungskurs

## What is this about? {#S1}

So far, we have considered individual functions and their derivatives or integrals. In advanced calculus, we now examine **whole families of functions** that differ by an adjustable parameter. Integrals also become more challenging when functions are combined, areas are bounded by two curves or a function graph generates a three-dimensional solid. The basic approach remains the same: clarify the question, establish the model, read the new symbols and only then calculate. Some selected techniques are explicitly included in the Berlin advanced-course (Leistungskurs) examination priorities for 2026; this does not mean that every variant shown here is required in every examination year.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Think of a graph as a system with an adjustable control: when you change a parameter, the curve shifts, stretches or changes shape. Instead of treating a completely new function for each parameter value, we examine the pattern of the entire **family of functions**. Similarly, integrals can be understood in terms of reusable procedures: a well-chosen new variable can simplify a nested expression. Areas and solids of revolution also connect calculus with geometry and 3D models.

## New symbols before the first formula {#S3}

| Symbol | Read aloud | Meaning here | Other possible uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| $f_a(x)$ | ‘eff sub a of x’ | Function from a family; a is the fixed chosen parameter | Subscripts also identify series of measurements |
| $a > 0$ | ‘a is greater than zero’ | Permitted positive parameter of the family | ‘a’ may otherwise be a limit of integration |
| $x^{2}$ | ‘x squared’ | Square of the variable x | A superscript digit can also denote another type of index |
| $u$ | ‘you’ | New integration variable in a substitution | Any other variable |
| $\mathrm{d}u$ | ‘dee u’ | Differential notation for the new integration variable | Explained more precisely in higher mathematics |
| $\mathrm{d}$ | ‘dee’ | Differential notation, e.g. $\mathrm{d}u$ and $\mathrm{d}x$ | Not itself an ordinary number |
| $\infty$ | ‘infinity’ | Unbounded limit of integration | In limit problems generally |
| $\lim$ | ‘limit’ | Limiting process when a bound grows indefinitely; from [M2-L2](M2-L2.md) | Limit of a sequence or function |
| $\pi$ | ‘pi’ | Circle constant, needed for circle areas and volumes | Already familiar from [M1-L15](M1-L15.md) |
| $V$ | ‘vee’ | Volume of a solid of revolution | Vector space, electrical voltage, etc. |
| $g(x)$ | ‘gee of x’ | Second function graph when dealing with areas between curves | Any other function |

## Families of functions: a curve with a free parameter {#S4}

A **family of functions** contains several functions differing only by one or more parameters. The parameter is **held fixed** for a particular curve, while the input $x$ varies along the graph. Example:

$$
f_a(x)=a\cdot x-x^2\qquad(a>0)
$$

**Read in full:** ‘Eff with subscript a of x equals a times x minus x squared, where a is greater than zero.’

Here $a$ is the positive parameter, $x$ is the variable input; $a\cdot x$ is a product and $x^{2}$ is the square of the input. Each chosen parameter value produces a separate downward-opening parabola. It has zeros at $x=0$ and $x=a$. From the derivative $f_a'(x)=a-2x$, we see that the curve reaches its highest point at $x=\frac a2$. Completing the square gives $f_a(x)=\frac{a^2}{4}-\left(x-\frac a2\right)^2$; since a square is never negative, the **global** maximum for all real $x$ is $\frac{a^2}{4}$. For $a>0$, this maximum point lies between the two zeros.

**Mini-example:** For $a=6$, the function is $6x-x^{2}$. Its zeros are at 0 and 6; the maximum is at $x=3$, with function value 9. A single symbolic differentiation therefore explains a whole family of graphs.

**Limitation of the model:** A parameter in a family is not treated simultaneously as a time variable. If $a$ itself is made dependent on $x$, the differentiation rules and the question change.

## Connecting parameter and area {#S5}

If we want to calculate the positive area under the graph of the family between its two zeros, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(a)&=\int_0^a(ax-x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac{a^3}{6}\quad(a>0)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The area capital A of a is the integral of a times x minus x squared with respect to x from zero to a; the result is a cubed divided by six.’

The capital letter $A$ denotes **area** here, whereas lower-case $a$ is the parameter of the family. The same letter in upper and lower case therefore has different roles here. When integrating with respect to $x$, $a$ remains constant. An antiderivative is therefore $\frac{ax^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}$. Substituting first $x=a$ and then $x=0$ gives $a^3\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)=\frac{a^3}{6}$.

For $a=6$, this gives $\frac{6^3}{6}=36$ **square units in the chosen coordinate representation**. Here we use $x$ and $f_a(x)$ as pure coordinate numbers. If physical units are introduced instead, the unit of each term of $a\cdot x-x^{2}$ and of the integral must be checked separately; the notation alone does not establish a real unit of measurement. Conversely, given a desired positive area $A$, the parameter $a={(6A)}^{1/3}$ can be determined, provided the coordinate and sign assumptions chosen here hold. Without the condition $a>0$, the statement ‘positive area between two zeros from 0 to a’ cannot be adopted in this simple form.

## Integration by linear substitution {#S6}

With a nested expression, it sometimes helps to temporarily denote the inner part by a new variable $u$. This is particularly simple if the inner function is linear, so its derivative is constant.

We consider:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\int_0^1 2{(2x+1)}^3\,\mathrm{d}x
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The integral of two times open bracket two x plus one close bracket cubed with respect to x from zero to one.’

The substitution is $u=2x+1$, read ‘u equals two x plus one’. In differential notation, $\mathrm{d}u=2\,\mathrm{d}x$, read ‘dee u equals two times dee x’. At $x=0$, we get $u=1$; at $x=1$, we get $u=3$. We must convert **both limits** if we write the definite integral wholly in the new variable.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=2x+1,\quad \mathrm{d}u=2\,\mathrm{d}x\\
x=0&\implies u=1,\quad x=1\implies u=3\\
\int_0^1 2{(2x+1)}^3\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_1^3 u^3\,\mathrm{d}u\\
&=\left[\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^3\\
&=\frac{81-1}{4}\\
&=20
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The integral from one to three of u cubed with respect to u is u to the fourth power divided by four, evaluated between one and three, and equals twenty.’

The transformed problem is simpler because the brackets have disappeared. We can check by differentiating the antiderivative $\frac{{(2x+1)}^4}{4}$; by the **chain rule**, its derivative is $\frac{4{(2x+1)}^3\cdot2}{4}=2{(2x+1)}^3$. The factor $2$ before the brackets is crucial here; if this factor were missing, the substitution would have to be adjusted accordingly.

**Limitation:** You must not perform a substitution and keep the old integration limits unchanged without checking them. Moreover, simple **linear** substitution is not a replacement for every more advanced method of integration. Integration by parts or special trigonometric substitutions are not automatically part of the Berlin 2026 examination requirements described here.

## Area between two graphs {#S7}

When two curves enclose an area, we count the vertical difference between the upper and lower graphs. If $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ denote the two heights on an interval, and $f(x) \geq g(x)$ holds there, then the area is:

$$
A=\int_a^b (f(x)-g(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad(f(x)\geq g(x)\text{ on the interval})
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The area A is the integral of eff of x minus gee of x with respect to x between a and b.’

The upper function really must be above the other throughout the interval being considered. If the graphs intersect, it may be necessary to split the interval at the intersections or integrate the absolute value of the difference. More precisely, for continuous functions on a finite interval the **total** non-negative area is $\int_a^b|f(x)-g(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x$; without the absolute value, component areas can cancel each other out. Absolute value notation was already explained in M2-L8.

**Mini-example:** Between $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=x^{2}$ in the interval from zero to one, $x$ is the upper graph. Therefore, $\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac12-\frac13=\frac16$ square units. In a 3D graphics program, a similar calculation could give an intersection area or a cross-sectional area of two mathematically defined profiles.

## Improper integrals: a limit lies at infinity {#S8}

Some areas extend indefinitely. This does **not automatically** mean that their area is infinite. We first examine the integral up to a finite upper limit and then take a limit.

$$
\int_1^\infty\frac1{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^b\frac1{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x=1
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The integral of one over x squared with respect to x from one to infinity is the limit, as b tends to infinity, of the integral from one to b; this limit is one.’

Here $\infty$, written at the upper right, is the infinite upper limit; in a simple text representation we can write `∫₁^∞` for clarity. For finite $b>1$, the antiderivative $-\frac1x$ gives the value $1-\frac1b$. As $b$ becomes arbitrarily large, $\frac1b$ approaches zero. The area therefore converges to one.

Not every improper integral converges. For $\frac1x$ starting at one, for example, the accumulated area grows without a finite limit, because the expression $\ln(b)$ grows without bound as $b\to \infty$. A symbol $\infty$ alone therefore **tells us nothing yet** about whether the result is finite. An **unbounded function at a finite limit** also leads to an improper integral. We denote an arbitrarily small **positive approaching limit** by $\varepsilon$ (‘epsilon’) and let it tend to zero from the right: $\int_0^1\frac1{\sqrt{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x$ means the limit of integrals from $\varepsilon$ to $1$. The antiderivative $2\sqrt{x}$ gives $2-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, or $2$ in the limit. In contrast, $\int_0^1\frac1x\,\mathrm{d}x$ diverges because its limit contains $-\ln(\varepsilon)$ and grows without bound. Here the notation `∫_ε^1` is used for the temporary positive lower limit.

## Solids of revolution: a curve generates a volume {#S9}

If **the region between the graph of a continuous non-negative function $f$ and the x-axis** on the interval from $a$ to $b$ (with $a\leq b$) is rotated about the x-axis, a **filled solid of revolution** is formed. Rotating only the graph line gives merely its curved surface, not its volume. The cross-section of the filled solid perpendicular to the x-axis is a circular disc whose radius corresponds to the function value. The area of the circle is $\pi\,\mathrm{radius}^2$ – familiar from M1-L15. Summing the thin circular discs gives the volume:

$$
V=\pi\cdot\int_a^b{[f(x)]}^2\,\mathrm{d}x
$$

**Read in full:** ‘The volume vee is pi times the integral of the square of eff of x with respect to x, from a to b.’

Here $V$ is the volume, $\pi$ is the circle constant, and ${[f(x)]}^2$ means **square the entire function value**, not just the variable. The limits determine the length of the section being rotated.

**Mini-example:** Rotating the **region between** the straight line $f(x)=x$ and the x-axis for $0\leq x\leq 2$ about the x-axis produces a cone with height two and radius two. The integral gives $\pi\int_0^2x^2\,\mathrm{d}x=\pi\cdot\frac83=\frac{8\pi}{3}$ cubic units. The cone formula familiar from geometry gives the same value.

**Limitation:** For hollow solids between two surfaces of revolution or rotations about other axes, adjusted formulae apply. The disc formula must not be applied to every 3D problem without checking it.

## What is actually included in Berlin's advanced course? {#S10}

The official **Mathematics Examination Priorities 2026, advanced course (Leistungskurs), pages 3–4**, mention, among other things, families of functions, limits, the connection between differentiation and integration, integration by linear substitution, calculations of areas, improper integrals, volumes of revolution and calculating parameters or integration limits for specified quantities. The particular classes of functions and restrictions described there must be taken into account. Our lesson presents a **selection aimed at understanding**, not an exhaustive list of examination requirements.

## What is it useful for? {#S11}

Families of functions are a basic tool in parametric modelling: a parameter might represent component size, a cost factor or an input threshold. Linear substitution is a useful way of thinking in symbolic computer algebra. Areas between curves help compare models; we encounter integrals to infinity later in probability and signal processing. Solids of revolution connect analytical formulae with 3D geometry, design and robotics.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S12}

- A **parameter** and a **running variable** must be clearly distinguished both in words and in calculations.
- Areas require the correct upper and lower graphs, or splitting at intersections; a signed integral is not always positive.
- Improper integrals are defined using limits. Some converge, others diverge.
- The formula for rotation using circular discs assumes the **filled region** between a continuous curve and the x-axis, the appropriate axis of rotation and the correct radius. A rotated graph alone describes a surface; for hollow solids, the difference between two circular areas may be needed.
- The scope of this lesson follows the **Berlin 2026 examination priorities for the advanced course (Leistungskurs)**. Other years, regions or degree courses must not be treated as equivalent without further checking.
- More complex techniques of integration, the theory of parameter dependence and general integration in several variables are covered only in advanced university studies.

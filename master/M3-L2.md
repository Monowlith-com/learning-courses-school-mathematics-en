# M3-L2 – Linear inequalities and intervals {#L2}

**Course area:** M3 – Expressions, equations and inequalities

**Level:** Lower secondary education · foundations

**Prior knowledge:** [M1-L3](M1-L3.md); [M1-L8](M1-L8.md)

**Context:** Core mathematics extension, informed by Bavaria’s LehrplanPLUS M8.

## Learning objectives {#S1}

You can solve linear inequalities and justify when the inequality sign reverses. You can represent solution sets as intervals and on number lines and restrict them to the appropriate number system in applied problems.

## Comparison and solution sets {#S2}

An equation requires equality, whereas an inequality compares quantities. $a<b$ means “a is less than b”, and $a\le b$ includes equality; $>$ and $\ge$ express the reversed comparisons. A linear inequality in one variable can be rearranged to $ax+b<0$ or one of the other three comparison forms. The coefficients $a,b$ are fixed real numbers; in the special case $a=0$, the variable disappears. The solution set contains all real inputs that make the comparison true.

## Equivalent transformations {#S3}

Adding the same number to both sides preserves the difference between the values. Multiplying or dividing by a positive number also preserves order. A negative factor reverses the order: multiplying $2<5$ by $-1$ gives $-2>-5$. Division by zero is forbidden; multiplying by zero is not reversible.

For example:

$$7-3x\le1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad-3x\le-6\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\ge2.$$

The first step subtracts 7 from both sides; the second divides by $-3$ and reverses the comparison. Check: at $x=2$, $1\le1$; at $x=3$, $-2\le1$; at $x=1$, $4\le1$ is false. These checks illustrate the result; the equivalent transformations prove it for all real numbers.

## Intervals and number lines {#S4}

A square bracket includes a finite endpoint; a round parenthesis excludes it. $\infty$ is not a real number, so an infinite endpoint always uses a parenthesis. On a number line a filled point is included and an open circle is excluded.

| Condition | Interval |
| --- | --- |
| $x\ge2$ | $[2,\infty)$ |
| $x<2$ | $(-\infty,2)$ |
| $-1<x\le4$ | $(-1,4]$ |
| all real numbers | $(-\infty,\infty)$ |
| no solution | $\varnothing$ |

![Figure: The interval $[2,\infty)$ with an included endpoint and $(-1,4]$ with an excluded and an included endpoint.](../media/img/S_MAT_034_EN.svg){alt="Two number lines: top extends right from 2 inclusive; bottom extends from minus 1 exclusive to 4 inclusive."}

## Multiple conditions and special cases {#S5}

For $-1<2x+1\le9$, both conditions must hold. Subtract 1 everywhere and divide by 2: $-1<x\le4$. The common solution set is $(-1,4]$. For $2x+1<2x+5$, subtracting $2x$ leaves the true statement $1<5$, so every real number is a solution. For $2x+5<2x+1$, the remaining statement is false; there is no solution. These cases explain why blindly dividing by a potentially zero coefficient is invalid.

## Application: a limited budget {#S6}

An event venue charges a base fee of 30 euros plus 4 euros per person. The budget is at most 78 euros. For $n$ people, $30+4n\le78$, so $n\le12$. The model variable is a nonnegative integer; the actual solution set is $\{0,1,\dots,12\}$. Twelve people cost 78 euros, thirteen cost 82 euros. A condition of “less than 78 euros” would exclude twelve. The result does not account for extra fees or a minimum number of attendees.

## Self-assessment {#S7}

### Exercises

1. Solve $5-2x>11$ and state the interval.
2. Solve $-3\le2x+1<7$ and mark the endpoints.
3. Solve $4(x+1)\ge4x+4$ and $4(x+1)<4x$.
4. Renting a device costs 12 euros plus 2.50 euros per hour. The budget is strictly less than 32 euros. How many whole hours are possible?
5. Explain why $x\cdot a<x\cdot b$ does not imply $a<b$ in general.

::: {.lc-solutions data-lc-section="S7"}
1. $-2x>6$, so $x<-3$; interval $(-\infty,-3)$.
2. $-4\le2x<6$, hence $-2\le x<3$; $[-2,3)$, left endpoint included, right excluded.
3. The first reduces to $4\ge4$, so $\mathbb R$. The second reduces to $4<0$, so $\varnothing$.
4. $12+2.5h<32$ gives $h<8$. With $h\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}$, 0 to 7 whole hours are possible, at most 7.
5. When $x>0$ the order stays the same; when $x<0$ it reverses. When $x=0$ the initial strict inequality is false, and division would be invalid. Example: $x=-1$, $a=5$, $b=2$; $-5<-2$, but $5>2$.
:::

## Connections and limitations {#S8}

[M1-L3](M1-L3.md) supplies algebraic transformations, and [M1-L8](M1-L8.md) covers linear functions. Graphically, $f(x)\le g(x)$ corresponds to the region in which the graph of $f$ is no higher than the graph of $g$. With rational expressions from [M3-L1](M3-L1.md), domain restrictions and changing signs also matter. General rational or quadratic inequalities are not solved in this lesson.

# M1-L7 – Powers, powers of ten and roots {#L7}

**Course section:** M1 – Lower secondary education (Sekundarstufe I)

**Level:** Lower secondary education

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Classification:** Lower secondary education, approximately Year 8 of German schooling · Format: quick revision

## What is this about? {#S1}

A power is a short way of writing repeated multiplication by the same number. It is useful when describing large numbers, very small measurements or regular growth. A root asks the reverse question: which number, multiplied by itself the required number of times, gives a particular value? Both are also important for quickly understanding technical orders of magnitude and mathematical formulae encountered later.

## Basic idea and illustration {#S2}

Imagine a square grid with four cells in each row and four rows: it contains 16 cells. Instead of ‘four times four’, we can write ‘four to the power of two’. In a cube with three cells in each spatial direction, it would be ‘three to the power of three’, giving 27 small cubes. The raised number describes **how many times** a factor is used; it is not simply multiplied by the factor.

## New symbols before the first formula {#S3}

| Symbol | Pronunciation | Meaning here | Other possible uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| $a$ | ay | The **base** of a power | Any variable or side length |
| $n$, $m$ | en, em | Integer **exponents** in the laws of indices | Counting numbers, indices or model parameters |
| $a^n$ or `a^n` | a to the power of n | Power with base a and exponent n; `^` is the plain-text notation | In many programming languages, `^` does **not** mean exponentiation |
| `·` | times | Multiplication | Later, for example, the scalar product |
| `√` | square root of | Non-negative square root of a non-negative number | A different type of root may have a small root index |
| `∛` | cube root of | Number whose cube gives the original value | More generally, roots of higher order |
| `≈` | is approximately equal to | Rounded or approximate numerical value | An approximation in general, not exact equality |
| `≠` | is not equal to | Excludes a value that is not allowed, such as zero as the base with a negative exponent | Inequality in general |
| `−` | minus | Negative sign or subtraction | Depends on its position |

## Most important formulae: powers {#S4}

**Formula 1:** $4^2=4\cdot4=16$

**Read aloud:** ‘Four to the power of two is four times four, which equals sixteen.’ The **4** is the base; the raised **2** is the exponent and indicates two factors. $4^3$ would be ‘four to the power of three’, or four times four times four, giving 64.

**Formula 2 – Multiplying powers with the same base:** $a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}$

**Read aloud:** ‘a to the power of m times a to the power of n equals a to the power of the sum of m and n.’ The exponents $m$ and $n$ are added **because** the numbers of equal factors are added. This works directly for positive integer exponents; for negative exponents, the base must not be zero.

**Mini-example:** $2^3\cdot2^2=2^5=32$. Read aloud: ‘Two to the power of three times two to the power of two equals two to the power of five, or thirty-two.’ There are five factors in all, each with the value two. This example assumes positive integer exponents; the general rule for arbitrary **integer** exponents requires $a\ne0$ when negative exponents are involved.

Two other frequently used rules in words:

- When dividing powers with **the same non-zero base**, subtract the exponents: $\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}$.
- When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents: ${(a^m)}^n=a^{m\cdot n}$; here the exponents are integers, and for negative exponents the rule applies only when $a\ne0$.

When the bases are different, we cannot simply combine the exponents. In particular, $2^3+2^2$ is an **addition** and is not the same as $2^5$.

### Zero and negative exponents {#abschnitt-5}

For a non-zero base: $a^0=1$. Read aloud: ‘a to the power of zero is one.’ We do not use this rule for $a=0$; $0^0$ depends on the context and is not defined here as an ordinary numerical power.

A negative integer exponent stands for the **reciprocal** of a positive power, provided $a\ne0$: $a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n}$ for a positive integer $n$. **Read aloud:** ‘a to the power of minus n is the reciprocal of a to the power of n.’ For example: $10^{-3}=\frac1{1000}=0.001$. Read aloud: ‘Ten to the power of minus three equals one thousandth, or zero point zero zero one.’ The exponent ‘minus three’ does **not** mean that the result is negative; with a negative base, the sign also depends on the exponent.

## Powers of ten: orders of magnitude at a glance {#S5}

Using powers of ten, we can write long numbers compactly:

| Number | Power notation | Read as |
|---|---|---|
| 1 000 | $10^3$ | ten to the power of three |
| 1 000 000 | $10^6$ | ten to the power of six |
| 0.01 | $10^{-2}$ | ten to the power of minus two |
| 0.000001 | $10^{-6}$ | ten to the power of minus six |

In **normalised scientific notation** a **non-zero** number is written using its sign, a factor and a power of ten. The **absolute value** of the factor lies between one (inclusive) and ten (exclusive). For a negative number the factor remains negative, for example $-4.2\cdot10^6$. Zero is written separately as $0$; it has no uniquely determined normalised power of ten. For example:

$4.2\cdot10^6=4\,200\,000$.

**Read aloud:** ‘Four point two times ten to the power of six equals four million two hundred thousand.’ The factor $4.2$ tells us *how many* millions; $10^6$ gives the order of magnitude. In many programming languages and file formats this is written, for example, as `4.2e6`; here `e6` is **exponent notation**, not Euler's number $e$, which is discussed later.

## Roots: the reverse question {#S6}

**Formula 3:** $\sqrt{25}=5$.

**Read aloud:** ‘The square root of twenty-five is five.’ Why? Because five times five is twenty-five. In standard notation, the square root symbol `√` refers to **the non-negative value**. Therefore $\sqrt{25}$ is **5**, not ‘plus or minus five’.

The **equation** $x^2=25$ is different: it has two solutions, $x=5$ and $x=-5$, because both give 25 when squared. So ‘What is the value of the square root?’ is not the same question as ‘Which numbers give 25 when squared?’

We must carefully distinguish signs and brackets: ${(-2)}^2=4$, because the negative number is squared; whereas $-2^2=-4$, because the two is squared first and the minus sign is applied afterwards. **Read aloud:** ‘Minus two in brackets squared equals four; minus two squared without brackets equals minus four.’ The cube root asks the same question for the third power: $\sqrt[3]{27}=3$, read ‘the cube root of twenty-seven is three’. Cube roots can also have negative results, for example $\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$.

Not every root is an integer: $\sqrt2\approx1.4142$. This result is rounded. Within the **real numbers** ([M1-L1](M1-L1.md)), the square root of a negative number is undefined. In a later lesson on complex numbers we extend the number system.

## Fully explained mini-example {#S7}

A storage system theoretically contains $4.2\cdot10^6$ records. This notation means $4\,200\,000$ records – a much easier order of magnitude to read. If the record count is to be displayed in a table, a program could format it as ‘4.2 million’. This is only another **representation** of the same number; it does not tell us the storage requirements or the actual available capacity.

## What is it useful for? {#S8}

Powers of ten help us read nanoseconds, file sizes, amounts of data and measurements. Powers occur in exponential growth, in counting possible combinations and later in estimating the computational cost of algorithms. Roots occur in distances in coordinate systems, statistical measures of spread and vector lengths. Recognising their notation helps us understand many technical formulae before we need to calculate them ourselves.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S9}

The laws of indices have conditions: zero is excluded as a base for negative integer exponents. Not every ordinary root is defined within the real numbers; for even-order roots, the number under the root sign must not be negative there. Power notation also varies between programming languages: Python, for example, uses `**` rather than `^` for powers, while `^` denotes a bitwise XOR operation. Very large or very small numbers cannot always be represented exactly in computer data types.
